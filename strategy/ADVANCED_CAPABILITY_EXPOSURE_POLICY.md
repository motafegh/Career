# Advanced Capability Exposure Policy

**Owner:** Ali Rajabi  
**Status:** Approved and controlling Career advanced-capability policy  
**Activated:** 2026-07-27  
**Responsibility:** Career-level admission, evidence, and claim rules for advanced systems, platforms, AI methods, security depth, and emerging technical areas

## 1. Purpose

Ali has legitimate long-term interest in advanced areas including:

- distributed queues and asynchronous systems;
- microservices and service boundaries;
- Kubernetes and orchestration;
- cloud and multi-cloud systems;
- MLOps;
- autonomous and multi-agent systems;
- graph and structural methods;
- advanced security engineering;
- additional ML and AI methods.

The Career system must preserve this ambition without forcing every technology into one project or treating exposure as professional mastery.

## 2. Governing principle

Advanced capability develops through:

```text
career or product question
→ simplest credible baseline
→ observed limitation or strategic gap
→ bounded exposure or pilot
→ real execution and failure evidence
→ comparison, cost, and ownership assessment
→ adopt, retain, reject, defer, or revisit
```

A technology name is not a career outcome.

No fixed quota requires all advanced areas to appear in the current flagship or current 90-day cycle.

## 3. Admission reasons

Advanced work may be admitted for one or more of these reasons:

- an active project has an observed limitation;
- a representative target role commonly requires the responsibility;
- a portfolio gap needs a bounded demonstration;
- a strategic comparison is necessary to choose a direction;
- an existing simpler method has measurable limitations;
- a career review identifies a high-value adjacent capability.

Curiosity alone may justify a short orientation or experiment, but not permanent architecture or a new major project.

## 4. Exposure levels

These A-levels describe advanced-technology exposure. They do not replace the D0–D5 capability model.

| Level | Name | Meaning | Minimum evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| A0 | Orientation | Understands the problem, main concepts, boundaries, and common trade-offs | Own-words explanation connected to a real responsibility |
| A1 | Hands-on exposure | Operates a bounded real or representative example and interprets behavior and one failure | Executable artifact or real operation, outputs, cleanup, assistance record, teach-back |
| A2 | Project-integrated pilot | Applies the technology to a representative project responsibility and compares it with a simpler baseline | Implementation, tests, measurements, failures, cost, and decision record |
| A3 | Adopted responsibility | Evidence justifies retaining the technology in a supported project or professional responsibility | Maintained integration, operation, tests, failure recovery, and ownership evidence |
| A4 | Independent advanced capability | Repeatedly designs, operates, diagnoses, and evolves the responsibility across changed contexts with low assistance | Multiple independent cases, unfamiliar failure diagnosis, consequential trade-offs, transfer evidence |

A1 or A2 may be a valid career outcome without A3 adoption. A rejected pilot may be strong evidence.

## 6. General admission gates

Before A2 or A3:

1. Identify the owning responsibility.
2. State the current baseline.
3. Provide evidence of the limitation or career need.
4. Define the smallest credible advanced alternative.
5. Define measurable success and rejection conditions.
6. Identify security, privacy, cost, maintenance, and cleanup consequences.
7. Ensure Ali has enough mental model to challenge the choice.
8. Keep the core product or existing responsibility operable where practical.
9. Record assistance and ownership requirements.
10. Decide explicitly after evidence.

## 7. Distributed queues and asynchronous systems

Relevant questions include:

- Which work is slow, rate-limited, retryable, bursty, or independently scalable?
- What delivery, acknowledgment, retry, duplication, ordering, backpressure, and dead-letter behavior exists?
- What does eventual completion mean to the user?
- How is idempotency preserved?

A1 may use a bounded producer-consumer example with one failure and cleanup. A2 requires a representative project workload and comparison with synchronous execution.

Do not retain a queue when simple synchronous or in-process work is clearer and sufficient.

## 8. Microservices and service boundaries

Relevant questions include:

- Is the responsibility independently deployable, scalable, secured, or owned?
- What network, versioning, data consistency, latency, and failure contracts appear?
- Is a modular monolith simpler and more reliable?

A1 may operate one small service boundary. A2 compares one representative extraction with the existing modular baseline.

Service count is not a success metric.

## 9. Kubernetes and orchestration

Relevant questions include:

- Does the workload contain multiple runtime components or operational lifecycle needs?
- What do deployments, services, jobs, configuration, secrets, health checks, rollout, and cleanup own?
- What does orchestration add beyond a local process or Docker Compose?

A1 may deploy a representative workload to a bounded local or low-cost environment, observe one failure or rollout, and clean it up.

A2 requires a project-integrated operational question. A3 requires sustained deployment or lifecycle value.

## 10. Cloud and multi-cloud

Relevant questions include:

- What deployment, identity, storage, networking, observability, or cost responsibility requires a provider?
- Which parts are portable and which are provider-specific?
- Does a second provider test portability, resilience, cost, or only create duplication?

Cloud work must include explicit cost and credential boundaries.

Multi-cloud is not a default requirement. A1 comparison may be sufficient; permanent multi-cloud requires a real operating need.

## 11. MLOps

MLOps work requires a defensible model lifecycle question:

- data and label identity;
- reproducible training or fitting;
- experiment and artifact tracking;
- evaluation gates;
- model promotion, replacement, or rollback;
- inference and monitoring;
- leakage, drift, and training-serving consistency.

Do not create an MLOps platform without a credible model, repeated lifecycle need, and simpler baseline.

A rigorous decision not to deploy or operationalize a weak model is valid evidence.

## 12. Agentic and multi-agent systems

Relevant questions include:

- Does the task contain genuinely separable roles or tool permissions?
- Does an agent improve quality or reduce effort over deterministic code or a controlled human workflow?
- How are sources, termination, tool authorization, shared state, conflict, cost, and recovery handled?

A2 must compare against a deterministic, single-agent, or human-controlled baseline.

Do not adopt autonomous target mutation or unrestricted tools for portfolio appearance.

## 13. Graph and structural methods

Relevant questions include:

- Does the problem contain relationships or paths that flat features lose?
- Can the structure be reproduced and evaluated?
- Does the representation improve a real decision or explanation?

Graph databases, graph models, and Graph Neural Networks are separate choices. None is implied by the existence of relationships.

## 14. Advanced security engineering

Security exposure must remain authorized, safe, lawful, and tied to defensive or trustworthy-engineering responsibilities.

Potential areas include:

- secure configuration and secrets;
- supply-chain and dependency evidence;
- input trust boundaries;
- threat modeling;
- logging and incident evidence;
- authentication and authorization;
- safe network-defense labs;
- vulnerability handling within approved boundaries.

Do not infer security-engineer readiness from one lab or from security-themed project branding.

## 15. Allocation strategy

Advanced exposure competes with core capability, portfolio, and market work.

During a cycle:

- protect employment-critical foundations first;
- protect the flagship's credible core;
- admit one substantial advanced exposure at a time by default;
- use the smallest allocation that can answer the question;
- stop when evidence is sufficient;
- reject or defer without treating the result as failure.

No percentage or count is mandatory across all cycles. `plans/CURRENT_CAREER_CYCLE.md` may authorize specific exposure outcomes when justified.

## 16. Career claims

Career and portfolio language must distinguish:

- orientation;
- hands-on operation;
- project-integrated pilot;
- adopted project responsibility;
- independently transferable capability.

Do not describe A1 or A2 as mastery, professional ownership, or production adoption.

## 17. Review triggers

Review an advanced area when:

- a project proposes adoption;
- a target-role pattern makes the capability materially important;
- a pilot ends;
- cost or risk changes;
- a portfolio claim is proposed;
- Ali considers specializing more deeply;
- evidence shows the area is artificial or distracting.

## 18. Change control

Change this policy only when admission reasons, A-level definitions, evidence requirements, allocation rules, or broad advanced-area claim standards change.

Specific project technology decisions belong in project repositories.
